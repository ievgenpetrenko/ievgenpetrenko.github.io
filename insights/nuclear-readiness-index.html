Nuclear Programme Strategy & Industrial Capability

Nuclear Readiness Index: Assessing Whether a Country Is Ready to Deliver and Sustain a Nuclear Programme

A strategic framework for assessing national capability across governance, regulation, workforce, quality infrastructure, supply chain, programme integration and climate resilience.

By Ievgen Petrenko | Published 4 October 2026, 18:56 CEST

Around the world, interest in nuclear energy is growing rapidly.

Governments are announcing new programmes, selecting reactor technologies, developing SMR strategies and entering into international partnerships. Nuclear power is expected to strengthen energy security, support decarbonisation and provide reliable low-carbon electricity for decades.

Yet one fundamental question deserves as much attention as technology selection, financing or construction schedules:

Is a country truly ready to deliver a successful nuclear programme?

Readiness is sometimes associated with political commitment, financing, site selection or the choice of reactor technology.

All of these matter.

But they are only part of a much larger system.

A nuclear programme ultimately depends on institutions, regulation, competent organisations, industrial capability, qualified people, financing, knowledge, resilient infrastructure and the ability to integrate all of them over decades.

A reactor technology can be selected.

A sustainable national nuclear capability has to be built.

Nuclear readiness already has a strong international foundation

Any discussion of national nuclear readiness should begin with the IAEA Milestones Approach.

The IAEA identifies 19 infrastructure issues that countries developing nuclear power need to address. They range from national position, nuclear safety, regulatory framework and human resource development to funding and financing, site and supporting facilities, industrial involvement, stakeholder involvement and procurement. The approach is organised through three phases and three corresponding milestones.

The IAEA also provides the Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review, INIR, a peer review service that helps Member States evaluate the status of their national nuclear infrastructure against the Milestones Approach and its 19 infrastructure issues.

This provides a comprehensive international foundation for national nuclear infrastructure development.

The Nuclear Readiness Index, NRI, proposed in this article serves a different purpose.

It is a strategic management concept designed to complement established nuclear infrastructure frameworks with a concise view of programme-level capability, dependency and maturity.

It is not an IAEA methodology. It is not a regulatory assessment tool. And it is not intended to replace the Milestones Approach, INIR, national self-assessment or project-specific regulatory evaluation.

Can the national system deliver and sustain what has been decided?

Eleven pillars of nuclear readiness

A useful Nuclear Readiness Index could examine eleven interconnected dimensions.

1. Strategic Governance

Does the country have a stable long-term nuclear strategy? Are responsibilities clearly defined between government, programme organisations, regulators, utilities and other institutions? Is there an effective mechanism for decisions that cross organisational boundaries?

Nuclear programmes extend far beyond individual political or corporate cycles. Without continuity of governance, even technically sound projects can lose alignment between policy, regulation, financing and delivery.

Strategic governance is therefore not simply about having a national nuclear policy. It is about maintaining the institutional capability to execute it.

2. Regulatory Readiness

Is the nuclear regulatory authority independent, competent and adequately resourced? Can it review complex reactor technologies, licensing documentation, safety analyses and project changes throughout the programme lifecycle?

Regulatory readiness cannot be created at the moment a licence application arrives. It requires technical competence, legal authority, organisational capacity and access to specialist support.

The regulator and the project organisation have different responsibilities, but their capabilities need to mature at a pace consistent with the programme.

3. Owner and Operator Capability

A nuclear programme needs a capable owner.

The owner organisation has to integrate engineering, licensing, procurement, quality, project controls, construction, commissioning and operational preparation.

Where the future operator is separate from the project owner, the interface becomes even more important. Operational capability cannot begin to develop only when construction is almost complete.

Knowledge of the plant, configuration, operating basis, maintenance requirements and safety case needs to move progressively into the future operating organisation.

Strong technology providers and EPC organisations are most effective when matched by a capable and progressively maturing owner organisation.

4. Talent Acquisition and Workforce Development

A nuclear programme requires people across many disciplines: engineers, licensing specialists, project managers, quality professionals, procurement specialists, inspectors, construction supervisors, commissioning personnel, operators and regulators.

The challenge is not simply the number of people. It is having the right competencies at the right time.

Some capabilities need to exist before major contracting decisions are made. Others become critical during engineering, construction, commissioning or operation.

Workforce planning therefore needs to follow the programme lifecycle rather than operate as a separate HR activity.

Do we have the competencies required for the next phase of the programme?

5. Quality Infrastructure

A national nuclear programme also depends on quality infrastructure beyond the nuclear installation itself.

This includes management systems, supplier qualification, testing and calibration capability, inspection arrangements, competent laboratories, conformity assessment and the ability to generate reliable objective evidence.

Quality cannot simply be added after contracts have been awarded. Organisations entering the programme need time to understand nuclear requirements, adapt their systems, develop competence and demonstrate implementation.

The important distinction is between having procedures and being able to execute controlled nuclear work.

6. Industrial and Supply Chain Capability

Localisation is increasingly a strategic objective of nuclear programmes.

Domestic industry can strengthen long-term capability, economic value, maintenance support and programme resilience. But participation should be matched to demonstrated readiness.

A capable industrial company does not automatically become a nuclear supplier because it enters a nuclear contract.

The programme needs to understand what domestic capability already exists, which scopes can realistically be localised, what qualification is required, where gaps remain, which international partnerships are needed and how suppliers can mature over successive units.

Localisation should therefore be treated as a capability-development process, not only as a percentage target.

7. Knowledge Management and Technology Transfer

A first nuclear project generates enormous amounts of knowledge. Some comes from technology providers and experienced international partners. Some is created inside the national programme itself.

If that knowledge remains primarily with individuals, consultants or temporary project teams, much of the programme's value can disappear when people leave.

Knowledge management therefore has to begin early.

A mature programme should understand which knowledge is strategically important, where it is held, how it will be transferred, who needs to receive it, how it will be retained and how experience from one unit will improve the next.

Technology transfer without organisational knowledge retention can create dependency rather than capability.

8. Financial and Commercial Readiness

Nuclear programmes require long-term financing and contracting structures capable of operating through uncertainty.

Financial readiness therefore extends beyond securing the initial capital. It includes procurement strategy, risk allocation, contract interfaces, inflation exposure, supply chain financing, contingency, change management and the long-term economics of the programme.

Commercial arrangements also affect technical behaviour. Poorly aligned contracts can encourage organisations to optimise individual packages while transferring risk to the wider programme.

Financial and commercial architecture should therefore support programme integration rather than work against it.

9. Programme Integration

This may be one of the most underestimated dimensions of nuclear readiness.

A programme can have a competent regulator, an experienced technology provider, strong contractors and capable suppliers and still struggle if those elements do not operate as one integrated system.

Engineering affects licensing. Licensing decisions affect design. Design changes affect procurement. Procurement affects construction. Construction evidence affects turnover and commissioning. Commissioning affects operational readiness.

Quality, configuration, requirements, schedules and decisions run through all of them.

Programme integration means maintaining these relationships as the project evolves.

  • Are requirements traceable across organisational boundaries?
  • Are interfaces clearly owned?
  • Are changes assessed before consequences propagate?
  • Does configuration remain aligned with the licensing and design basis?
  • Can decisions be translated into controlled execution?

The more organisations participate in a nuclear programme, the more important this capability becomes.

10. Stakeholder Confidence

A nuclear programme also depends on sustained confidence.

Government, regulators, industry, local communities, investors, employees and the public do not evaluate the programme from the same perspective.

But all of them need confidence that important decisions are being made competently, transparently and with nuclear safety appropriately protected.

Stakeholder confidence cannot be created only through communications. It comes from credible institutions, predictable decisions, visible competence and consistent delivery.

Trust is ultimately an outcome of how the programme operates.

11. Site, Infrastructure and Climate Resilience

A nuclear power programme is expected to operate for many decades.

The assumptions used today for siting, supporting infrastructure, external hazards, cooling, grid connections and emergency arrangements therefore need to remain credible well into the future.

Climate change adds another dimension to that challenge.

The IAEA has highlighted that nuclear infrastructure will increasingly need to account for changing climate hazards, including extreme temperatures, flooding, drought, water availability and compound extreme events.

National readiness should therefore consider more than whether a site and its infrastructure are acceptable under today's conditions.

It should ask whether the wider nuclear system remains robust as conditions evolve.

  • Are future external hazards adequately considered?
  • Can cooling requirements remain reliably supported?
  • Is critical transport and electrical infrastructure sufficiently resilient?
  • Can emergency arrangements function under severe external disruption?
  • Are important dependencies outside the nuclear site understood?
  • Are monitoring and periodic reassessment arrangements capable of identifying changes early enough?

A nuclear plant may be technically robust while still depending on vulnerable external systems.

For a programme expected to operate over generations, climate and infrastructure resilience should therefore be treated as part of strategic nuclear readiness, not as an isolated environmental issue.

The pillars cannot be assessed independently

These eleven dimensions are tightly connected.

Weak governance can slow decision-making. Insufficient regulatory capability can affect licensing schedules. Licensing uncertainty can delay procurement. Supply chain weakness can affect construction. Workforce gaps can appear later during commissioning. Poor knowledge transfer can weaken owner and operator capability. External infrastructure vulnerabilities can affect construction or operation even when the plant itself is robust.

A weakness in one area rarely stays within that area.

This is why readiness should not be reduced to eleven independent scores and a simple average.

The real value lies in understanding dependencies.

A country may have a strong overall profile while still carrying a critical weakness that constrains the entire programme.

Some dimensions therefore function more like programme gates than ordinary indicators.

How could a Nuclear Readiness Index work?

The NRI should not create an illusion of mathematical precision.

Its purpose should be to make capability, maturity and dependency visible.

Each pillar could be assessed using a simple maturity model:

  • 1. Initiated - The need has been recognised and initial actions have begun.
  • 2. Defined - Responsibilities, structures and plans are established.
  • 3. Developing - Capability is being built and implemented.
  • 4. Demonstrated - The relevant organisations can provide evidence that the capability works.
  • 5. Sustainable - The capability is embedded, repeatable and capable of supporting future programme phases or additional units.

The resulting output would be more useful as a readiness profile than as a league table.

The objective is not to say: Country A scores 82 and Country B scores 76.

The objective is to identify where a programme is mature, where capability development is progressing and where unresolved gaps could affect the next major commitment.

Readiness is phase-dependent

A country does not need every capability to be fully mature on day one.

That would be unrealistic.

What matters is whether capability develops ahead of the point at which the programme depends on it.

This is consistent with the logic of the IAEA Milestones Approach, which develops nuclear infrastructure progressively through successive phases rather than treating readiness as a single event.

Are the capabilities required for the next phase developing quickly enough?

The framework should drive action

The value of a readiness assessment is not the score.

It is the action that follows.

A gap in regulatory capability may require recruitment, training or technical support. A weakness in industrial readiness may require supplier development and qualification. A knowledge-management gap may require structured transfer from technology providers and experienced partners.

A programme-integration weakness may require stronger interface ownership, requirements management or configuration control. A workforce gap may require action years before those people are needed. A climate-resilience gap may require further site assessment, infrastructure reinforcement or adaptation of long-term planning assumptions.

The framework becomes useful when it converts broad concerns into specific capability-development priorities.

Large reactors and SMRs face the same fundamental challenge

SMRs may change project size, deployment sequence, manufacturing strategy and financing models.

They do not eliminate the need for national infrastructure and institutional capability.

The latest IAEA Milestones guidance includes specific considerations for SMRs while retaining the broader infrastructure framework.

A country considering SMRs still needs governance, regulation, competent ownership, financing, workforce capability, supply chain development, knowledge management and programme integration.

The implementation model may change.

The fundamental readiness challenge remains.

Reactors can be purchased. Capability cannot.

Perhaps the most important value of a Nuclear Readiness Index is that it changes the focus of the discussion.

Nuclear programmes naturally attract attention to technology.

Which reactor? Which vendor? Which site? What capacity? What construction schedule?

These are necessary questions.

But technology alone does not create a sustainable nuclear programme.

Reactors can be purchased.

Long-term national capability cannot.

It has to be developed deliberately through institutions, organisations, people, industrial capability, knowledge, infrastructure and experience.

The countries that succeed will not simply be those that select good technologies. They will be those that build the national system capable of delivering, operating and sustaining them.

Bottom line

The global expansion of nuclear energy should not be measured only by how many reactors are announced or ordered.

A more meaningful measure will be how many programmes can move from political ambition to controlled execution and then to safe, reliable operation over generations.

The IAEA Milestones Approach provides a comprehensive infrastructure foundation for that journey.

A Nuclear Readiness Index could complement that foundation by giving senior decision-makers a concise strategic view across eleven interconnected dimensions: governance, regulation, owner capability, workforce, quality infrastructure, industry and supply chain, knowledge, finance, programme integration, stakeholder confidence, and site, infrastructure and climate resilience.

Not to rank countries.

Not to replace established international assessment mechanisms.

But to make one question harder to avoid:

Before committing to the next major step, are the capabilities needed to deliver and sustain it actually in place?

Before asking:

Which reactor should we build?

there is an equally important question:

Are we ready to deliver and sustain a successful nuclear programme?

That may be one of the most important indicators of all.

The Nuclear Readiness Index presented here is an independent strategic management concept proposed by the author. It is not an IAEA methodology, a regulatory assessment tool or a substitute for the IAEA Milestones Approach, INIR, national self-assessment or project-specific regulatory evaluation.
