Terminology Trap in Nuclear Projects: Why “Important to Safety” Is Not the Same as “Safety-Related”

Similar nuclear terminology can carry very different regulatory consequences. The distinction matters for classification, quality assurance, procurement, supplier requirements and licensing.

By Ievgen Petrenko | Published 4 October 2026

Nuclear projects increasingly operate across regulatory systems, technologies, standards and national supply chains.

That creates a risk which is easy to underestimate.

The same words can appear to mean the same thing while carrying different regulatory consequences.

One of the clearest examples is the relationship between terms such as “important to safety” and “safety-related.”

They are connected. They are not interchangeable.

Treating them as simple translations can affect safety classification, quality requirements, procurement, supplier qualification, commercial-grade dedication, documentation and ultimately licensing.

For an international nuclear programme, terminology is therefore not an editorial issue. It is part of regulatory integration.

The first mistake: translating words instead of regulatory concepts

A nuclear project may bring together an internationally developed reactor design, a national nuclear regulator, IAEA safety standards, technology-provider requirements, national legislation, engineering codes, quality assurance requirements and a supply chain that may have limited previous nuclear experience.

Each of these systems brings its own terminology.

The temptation is to create a simple dictionary:

Important to Safety = Safety-Related

That is where problems can begin.

A regulatory mapping needs to answer a different question: What function does a particular category perform in the originating framework, and what regulatory treatment is required when that concept is applied in another jurisdiction?

That requires understanding the logic behind the terminology, not only the terminology itself.

The IAEA approach: classification by safety significance

The IAEA framework uses the concept of items important to safety broadly.

Structures, systems and components are identified and classified according to their safety functions and their significance to nuclear safety.

This creates a graded approach.

The objective is not simply to divide the plant into two categories. It is to understand how different SSCs contribute to safety and to apply requirements proportionately to that contribution.

Factors may include the safety function performed, the consequences of failure, the circumstances in which the function is required and the role of the item in preventing or mitigating events.

Different safety classes can therefore lead to different expectations for design, qualification, reliability, verification, procurement and quality assurance.

The principle is straightforward: greater safety significance requires greater assurance. But the implementation is graded.

The NRC approach: a specific regulatory category

Within the U.S. NRC framework, safety-related has a specific regulatory meaning.

The term is associated with structures, systems and components relied upon to perform defined functions during and following design-basis events.

These functions include maintaining reactor coolant pressure boundary integrity, achieving and maintaining safe shutdown, and preventing or mitigating accident consequences.

This is more specific than saying that an item has some relationship to nuclear safety.

The broader expression important to safety can also appear within U.S. nuclear regulation, including contexts extending beyond the subset classified as safety-related.

That distinction is important. Even within one regulatory environment, important to safety does not automatically mean safety-related.

When technologies move between jurisdictions, the need for careful mapping becomes even greater.

The host-country framework adds another layer

When a reactor technology developed under one regulatory framework is deployed in another country, the receiving jurisdiction brings its own legal requirements, classification methodology, regulatory expectations and licensing processes.

Those requirements have to be integrated with the established design basis.

This does not mean replacing one classification system with another. It means establishing a controlled and defensible relationship between them.

A classification assigned within one regulatory framework should not be assumed to have an identical regulatory consequence in another jurisdiction without an established mapping of functions, definitions and applicable requirements.

Likewise, an item described as important to safety under an IAEA-based or national framework should not automatically be assigned requirements associated with the U.S. safety-related category.

The mapping should therefore begin with the safety function, regulatory definition and applicable design basis. The terminology comes after that.

Why this matters when reactor technologies cross regulatory jurisdictions

International nuclear programmes increasingly combine established reactor technologies with new host-country regulatory environments and local supply chains.

Each participating organisation may be working from a different point of reference.

The technology provider has an established design basis and licensing history. The national regulator applies domestic legislation and regulatory expectations. The project organisation has to demonstrate compliance within the host country. The EPC organisation has to translate these requirements into engineering, procurement and construction controls. Suppliers need to understand which requirements apply to their scope and why.

All of those interfaces depend on the same classification logic remaining intact.

This is why regulatory translation cannot be reduced to terminology alone.

The important question is not:

What is the closest equivalent word?

It is:

What safety function is being protected, what regulatory requirement follows from it, and how is that requirement controlled through project execution?

The quality assurance consequence

Classification has direct consequences for quality assurance.

Where safety significance drives specific QA requirements, that logic has to be reflected in procurement, supplier qualification, verification, traceability and acceptance.

If classification is mapped too broadly, controls may be imposed where they are not justified. That can result in unnecessary supplier qualification, additional documentation, excessive inspection, higher procurement cost, longer lead times and reduced supplier competition.

Mapping too narrowly creates the opposite risk.

Important controls may be omitted. Verification may be insufficient. Traceability may be incomplete. Procurement requirements may not reflect the intended safety function. Objective evidence may not support the required conclusion.

The issue therefore works in both directions. Over-classification can burden the project. Under-classification can weaken assurance.

The objective is not to maximise the number of nuclear controls. The objective is to apply the right controls to the right scope.

NQA-1 depends on a clear classification basis

NQA-1 provides a framework for controlling activities that affect nuclear quality. It does not remove the need for a clear classification basis.

Before QA requirements can be applied correctly, the project needs to understand what is being classified, what safety function it supports, what regulatory basis applies and which requirements have to flow down.

safety function → classification → applicable requirements → QA controls → procurement and verification evidence

Starting at the QA end of that chain can create either unnecessary controls or gaps in assurance.

Strong nuclear quality therefore begins before inspection, documentation or auditing. It begins with a clear technical and regulatory basis.

The problem reaches the supply chain

This is where terminology moves from regulatory interpretation into project delivery.

A supplier may receive requirements originating from the reactor design, technology provider, EPC organisation, national legislation, regulator, engineering codes and project quality programme.

Those requirements may all be technically valid.

But where regulatory mapping is not made explicit before requirements are flowed down, suppliers can receive technically correct requirements without understanding the logic that connects them.

That makes execution harder.

Questions begin to appear:

  • Why does this item require additional QA controls?
  • Why is this characteristic critical?
  • Why does this supplier require qualification?
  • Why does one component require additional traceability while another apparently similar component does not?
  • Why is commercial-grade dedication required in one case and not another?

Those questions cannot be answered effectively with “Because it is nuclear.”

They need a traceable technical and regulatory basis.

This becomes even more important as localisation expands. A broader national supply chain increases the number of organisational interfaces. That increases the need for requirements to remain understandable, traceable and consistent as they move from design and licensing into procurement and execution.

What a practical regulatory mapping should establish

  1. What safety function is involved? Start with the function, not the terminology.
  2. What does the originating framework actually mean? Understand the regulatory definition and design basis behind the category.
  3. What does the receiving jurisdiction require? Identify the national legal and regulatory treatment that applies.
  4. What engineering and quality requirements follow? Translate classification into design, procurement, qualification, verification, testing and documentation requirements.
  5. Can the logic be traced? An engineer, supplier, auditor or regulator should be able to follow the chain from safety function to classification and from classification to applicable controls.

If that chain cannot be demonstrated, the terminology has not really been resolved.

This is an integration issue

The deeper lesson is broader than one pair of terms.

International nuclear projects contain many similar interfaces: safety classification, seismic categories, quality classifications, equipment qualification, codes and standards, supplier requirements, documentation categories and configuration controls.

Each interface creates the possibility of false equivalence.

That is why a nuclear programme needs more than translation. It needs regulatory integration.

Regulatory integration means understanding the intent of different frameworks and establishing controlled relationships between them.

Engineering then has to preserve those relationships in the design. Quality has to preserve them through procurement and verification. Project controls have to preserve them when requirements or configurations change. The supply chain has to understand enough of the logic to execute the requirements correctly.

This is how regulatory intent becomes project evidence.

Bottom line

The words may look similar. The regulatory consequences may be very different.

“Important to safety” and “safety-related” should therefore not be mapped simply because the language appears equivalent.

Start with the safety function. Understand the regulatory definition. Establish the classification basis. Determine the engineering and quality controls that follow. Then maintain traceability through design, procurement, construction and commissioning.

For nuclear projects delivered across regulatory jurisdictions, terminology is part of requirements management, configuration control, licensing strategy and programme integration.

Getting that relationship right early is considerably easier than trying to reconstruct it later in procurement, construction or licensing.

This article discusses general regulatory integration principles using public regulatory and safety-standard sources. It does not assess or comment on any specific licensing decision or project-specific classification.
