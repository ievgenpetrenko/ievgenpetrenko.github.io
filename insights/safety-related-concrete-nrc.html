Safety-Related Concrete in NRC-Licensed Nuclear Facilities: More Than Just Strength

Why safety-related concrete is demonstrated through engineering control, qualified materials, controlled execution and traceable evidence, not strength alone.

By Ievgen Petrenko | Published 4 October 2026, 19:47 CEST

Concrete is one of the most important construction materials in a nuclear facility.

But in safety-related applications, concrete is not simply a structural material.

It can support safety-classified systems, protect equipment from external hazards, provide biological shielding and contribute to the integrity of structures that must continue to perform their required functions under normal, abnormal and accident conditions.

That changes the question.

The issue is no longer simply:

Does the concrete achieve its specified compressive strength?

The real question is:

Can the complete concrete system be demonstrated to perform its intended safety function throughout the required service life?

In an NRC-licensed nuclear project, the answer depends on much more than a cylinder test.

Safety-related concrete sits inside a regulatory system

The design and construction of nuclear concrete structures in the United States are governed by a combination of regulations, endorsed codes, quality assurance requirements and project-specific licensing commitments.

NRC Regulatory Guide 1.142 addresses safety-related concrete structures for nuclear power plants other than reactor vessels and containments and provides guidance for the application of ACI 349.

ACI 349 covers concrete structures that support, house or protect nuclear safety-class systems, while excluding concrete reactor vessels and concrete containment structures covered by ASME BPVC Section III, Division 2.

Quality assurance is another layer.

10 CFR Part 50 Appendix B establishes QA requirements for activities affecting safety-related structures, systems and components, and NRC guidance endorses versions of ASME NQA-1 for implementing QA programmes during design and construction, with defined exceptions and clarifications.

This distinction matters.

The structural code defines what the structure must achieve. The QA framework defines how confidence in that achievement is established and maintained.

Quality starts before concrete reaches the site

By the time concrete is placed, many important quality decisions have already been made.

They include:

  • design requirements,
  • material specifications,
  • mix qualification,
  • supplier qualification,
  • procurement requirements,
  • laboratory capability,
  • batch plant controls,
  • inspection arrangements,
  • personnel qualification,
  • testing methods,
  • acceptance criteria,
  • record requirements.

If these controls are weak, additional inspection at the point of placement cannot fully compensate for them.

This is one of the central differences between conventional construction quality and nuclear quality assurance.

The objective is not simply to detect defective work. It is to create a controlled process capable of producing conforming work consistently.

Materials are part of the safety case

Concrete performance begins with its constituents.

Cementitious materials, aggregates, water, admixtures and reinforcement have to meet the applicable design and procurement requirements.

But compliance is not just a matter of receiving a certificate.

The project needs confidence that the material delivered is the material specified.

That brings in:

  • approved suppliers,
  • procurement controls,
  • material identification,
  • traceability,
  • testing,
  • storage conditions,
  • handling,
  • change control,
  • acceptance documentation.

For safety-related work, purchased material and services are subject to QA controls because the integrity of the finished structure depends on the integrity of the supply chain behind it.

Mix qualification is about performance, not only strength

Compressive strength is essential.

It is not the entire performance envelope.

Depending on the design and structure, mix qualification may need to demonstrate characteristics such as:

  • strength,
  • workability,
  • durability,
  • density,
  • temperature behaviour,
  • shrinkage,
  • compatibility of constituents,
  • long-term performance.

For nuclear structures, environmental and service conditions can remain relevant for many decades.

NRC research on nuclear concrete durability highlights ageing mechanisms and environmental effects that can influence long-term structural performance.

That is why mix design is part of engineering control rather than merely a construction convenience.

Shielding concrete adds another performance dimension

Some concrete structures perform radiological shielding functions in addition to structural functions.

Where the design basis or project specification requires it, qualification may therefore extend beyond conventional structural properties.

The project may need to verify parameters such as:

  • concrete density,
  • elemental composition,
  • gamma attenuation characteristics,
  • neutron shielding characteristics,
  • constituent characteristics relevant to shielding performance.

In some specialised applications, radiological characterisation of raw materials can also be required by the project specification.

The important point is not that every nuclear concrete mix requires the same radiological testing.

It is that the verification programme has to reflect the actual safety and shielding functions assigned to the structure.

Construction execution is part of the quality system

A qualified mix can still produce an unacceptable structure if execution is not controlled.

The quality chain continues through:

  • batching,
  • transport,
  • placement,
  • consolidation,
  • temperature control,
  • curing,
  • reinforcement installation,
  • embedments,
  • construction joints,
  • inspection,
  • testing.

Each step can affect final performance.

This is why nuclear concrete construction requires defined responsibilities, approved procedures, qualified personnel and hold or witness points where appropriate.

NQA-1 has historically included specific requirements for installation, inspection and testing of structural concrete, structural steel, soils and foundations in nuclear power plants.

The structure is not accepted because the work looked good. It is accepted because the required controls were implemented and the evidence supports the conclusion.

Laboratories matter more than many projects expect

Testing laboratories are a critical interface.

Concrete test results may influence acceptance decisions with direct implications for safety-related structures.

That means confidence is required not only in the test result, but in the system that produced it.

Important controls can include:

  • personnel competence,
  • approved methods,
  • calibration,
  • equipment control,
  • sample identification,
  • chain of custody,
  • environmental conditions,
  • result review,
  • record retention.

A test result without reliable traceability is weaker evidence than it appears.

In nuclear construction, laboratory capability becomes part of project assurance.

Traceability turns construction into evidence

Nuclear construction creates a permanent record of how the plant was built.

That record may include:

  • material certificates,
  • batch records,
  • placement records,
  • inspection reports,
  • test results,
  • calibration certificates,
  • personnel qualification records,
  • nonconformance reports,
  • corrective actions,
  • design changes,
  • acceptance documentation.

These are not administrative by-products.

They are part of the evidence that the structure conforms to its design and licensing basis.

NRC Appendix B requires control over activities affecting safety-related SSCs, including design, procurement, construction, inspection and testing.

This is why traceability is not the same as document control.

The purpose is not merely to retain documents.

It is to preserve the relationship between:

requirement → material → activity → inspection → test → deviation → disposition → final acceptance

Nonconformance management is part of structural assurance

Concrete construction will not always proceed exactly as planned.

There may be:

  • out-of-tolerance results,
  • placement interruptions,
  • material deviations,
  • curing issues,
  • embedded item discrepancies,
  • test failures,
  • documentation gaps.

The important question is not whether deviations occur.

It is how they are controlled.

A mature nuclear QA system requires conditions adverse to quality to be identified, evaluated and corrected through controlled processes.

A nonconformance therefore cannot simply disappear through field acceptance.

Its technical significance has to be evaluated.

Its disposition has to be authorised.

And the final configuration has to remain aligned with the approved design basis.

Independence matters

Another key difference in nuclear construction is independent verification.

The person performing the work should not be the only source of confidence that the work is acceptable.

Inspection and testing provide independent confirmation that specified requirements have been met.

This creates separation between:

execution and acceptance

That separation is one of the foundations of nuclear quality assurance.

It becomes particularly important when construction pressure increases, schedules tighten and multiple organisations are working across the same interfaces.

Strength is only one data point

A concrete cylinder can demonstrate compressive strength.

It cannot demonstrate by itself that:

  • the correct material was used,
  • reinforcement was installed correctly,
  • embedments are in the right configuration,
  • curing requirements were met,
  • testing equipment was calibrated,
  • deviations were properly resolved,
  • records remain traceable,
  • the structure conforms to the licensing basis.

That is why safety-related concrete cannot be reduced to a test result.

The real product is not simply hardened concrete.

It is:

a constructed, verified and documented safety-related structure whose conformity can be demonstrated

Long-term performance matters

Nuclear facilities are designed for long operating lives.

Concrete therefore has to remain capable of performing under ageing, environmental exposure and potentially harsh service conditions.

NRC research has examined long-term degradation mechanisms and radiation-related ageing of concrete structures because performance has to be maintained over decades, not only demonstrated at initial acceptance.

This changes how quality should be understood.

Quality is not simply:

Was the concrete acceptable on the day it was poured?

It is also:

Was the material, design, construction and evidence base sufficiently controlled to support confidence in long-term performance?

Bottom line

Safety-related concrete in an NRC-licensed nuclear facility is more than concrete with a high specified strength.

Its assurance depends on the entire system surrounding it:

  • design control,
  • qualified materials,
  • supplier oversight,
  • mix qualification,
  • controlled batching and placement,
  • inspection and testing,
  • laboratory capability,
  • traceability,
  • nonconformance management,
  • configuration control,
  • permanent quality records.

The strength result matters.

But strength alone does not demonstrate nuclear quality.

Nuclear quality is demonstrated when engineering requirements are translated into controlled construction activities and those activities generate objective evidence showing that the structure can perform its intended safety function.

That is why, in nuclear construction, every cubic metre of safety-related concrete is part of a much larger assurance system.

And that system is what ultimately gives the project confidence that the structure will perform when it matters most.

This article discusses general nuclear construction and quality assurance principles using public NRC, ACI and ASME sources. Specific requirements depend on the applicable licensing basis, design classification, codes, project specifications and approved quality assurance programme.
