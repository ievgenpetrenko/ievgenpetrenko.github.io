Nuclear Plants Will Not Be Built by Nuclear Experts Alone

From industrial participation to repeatable nuclear delivery capability.

By Ievgen Petrenko | 9 October 2026 | Strategic Insight
The programme-level question

Can the industrial capability created for the first nuclear project be retained, improved and used again for the next one?

When a country decides to build its first nuclear power plant, attention naturally turns to reactor technology, financing, regulation, licensing and the availability of nuclear specialists. These are foundational requirements. But none of them, taken alone, creates an industry capable of delivering a nuclear programme.

Nuclear projects depend on a much wider industrial base: civil engineering and construction companies, equipment manufacturers, laboratories, logistics providers, designers, procurement teams, infrastructure contractors and specialist service companies.

Most of these organisations do not need to become nuclear organisations in every part of their business. They need to understand the requirements applicable to their assigned scope, demonstrate the necessary capability and work within an integrated nuclear delivery system.

The strategic objective is not simply to bring more companies into nuclear. It is to create the conditions in which capable companies can participate responsibly, deliver reliably and remain competitive beyond their first contract.

Industrial participation is not the same as industrial readiness

A company may be a successful bridge builder, industrial manufacturer or engineering contractor and still encounter unfamiliar obligations on a nuclear project. Depending on the scope, these may include controlled design inputs, supplier qualification, material traceability, inspection and test plans, management of nonconformances, calibration, configuration control, records retention and clearly defined acceptance authority.

Some of these practices are common in mature non-nuclear industries. Others have specific nuclear safety, quality or licensing implications. Their applicability must follow the actual regulatory and contractual requirements, the importance of the item or activity to safety, and the project's approved approach to classification and procurement.

A strong industrial supplier is not necessarily nuclear-ready. But nuclear readiness should not be confused with applying the most demanding nuclear controls to every contract indiscriminately.

Over-specification can add unnecessary cost and exclude capable suppliers. Under-specification can undermine safety assurance, evidence and delivery predictability. The right objective is proportionate, demonstrable and contractually clear capability.

Where industrial readiness becomes a programme delivery issue

Supplier development is often described as a procurement or quality activity. In practice, it cuts across the entire programme.

Capability areaProgramme-level consequence if not aligned
EngineeringUnclear requirements or immature interfaces are transferred into contracts, manufacturing and construction.
Qualification and qualitySuppliers may complete work but be unable to provide acceptable objective evidence of conformity.
ProcurementLong-lead items, supplier capacity and qualification lead times may not match the execution schedule.
ConstructionAccess, sequencing, inspections, handovers and configuration changes can create expensive field rework.
Licensing interfacesDocuments or changes relevant to the safety case may not be identified and managed early enough.
Knowledge retentionCapability acquired on the first unit may be lost before it benefits subsequent units.

These consequences rarely stay within a single organisation. Weakness in one contract can affect another contractor's workfront, the owner's decision-making, the EPC schedule or the evidence required for a later project milestone.

This is why national industrial development must be connected to engineering maturity, procurement strategy, regulatory commitments, project controls and construction planning. It cannot be managed only as a local-content target.

Five capabilities that must mature together

1. Scope-based supplier readiness

Companies need a realistic route from general industrial competence to the level of assurance required for their specific nuclear scope. This route may include gap assessments, competence development, qualification, surveillance, trial packages and clear evidence requirements. It should recognise the distinction between safety-related and other scope while respecting the applicable licensing basis.

2. Requirements and interface management

The wider the industrial participation, the more critical the interfaces. Requirements must remain understandable and traceable across designers, purchasers, subcontractors, testing organisations and site teams. Clear responsibilities and controlled changes prevent technical misunderstandings from becoming programme-level risks.

3. Quality infrastructure, not only quality procedures

Procedures alone cannot demonstrate delivery capability. Programmes also need competent inspectors, calibrated equipment, credible laboratories, reliable records, supplier oversight and the ability to resolve deviations before they propagate. Quality supports engineering, construction and licensing; it is not a substitute for any of them.

4. Workforce and organisational learning

Supplier development must cover the people who actually execute the work: engineers, foremen, welders, planners, laboratory personnel, purchasers, document specialists and managers. Their learning should survive staff turnover and contract completion. This requires structured knowledge transfer and repeatable work practices, not only one-off courses.

5. Programme-level integration and governance

Owners, technology providers, EPC organisations and suppliers have distinct responsibilities. Governments shape enabling industrial policy and infrastructure. Independent nuclear regulators establish and enforce safety requirements within their statutory remit. Their independence must not be compromised in the name of project coordination.

Programme integration is the disciplined coordination of delivery interfaces, decisions, evidence and dependencies among accountable organisations, without diluting their respective responsibilities.

The capability progression

Industrial competence → Scope-specific nuclear readiness → Demonstrated delivery → Retained capability → Repeatable performance.

Localisation should increase capability, not just local spending

National participation can create jobs, support businesses and build industrial resilience. Those are important outcomes. But percentage-based localisation alone does not reveal whether a programme is generating transferable technical capability.

For an established manufacturer, a first nuclear contract might require investment in specific testing, traceability or inspection practices. For a construction contractor, the decisive investment may be in interface control, field documentation and supervisory competence. For a laboratory, it may be equipment, personnel qualification or technical scope expansion.

The most valuable outcome is not a company that can deliver one nuclear work package under exceptional supervision. It is a company that can perform similar work again, improve its processes and qualify for increasingly demanding roles where justified.

That does not mean every component must be produced domestically. International partnerships, imported specialist equipment and cross-border supply chains will remain essential. Effective localisation means choosing a realistic national role, developing it deliberately and connecting it to the wider programme.

From the first unit to a fleet

The first nuclear unit carries a special burden: a country has to create institutions, organisations, relationships and industrial processes while also delivering a complex construction project. If every subsequent unit begins with a new qualification effort, a new knowledge base and a new set of unresolved interfaces, the programme is failing to capture its own learning.

Fleet deployment changes the economics of readiness. More stable engineering, qualified repeat suppliers, retained competence, lessons learned and consistent interfaces can make later projects more predictable. But this benefit is not automatic. Design modifications, new sites, changing organisations and changes in regulatory expectations still need appropriate review and control.

The same principle applies to small modular reactors. Factory fabrication and standardisation may change how work is distributed. They do not remove the need for qualified suppliers, consistent configuration, reliable evidence, regulatory oversight or a capable owner.

Industrial readiness becomes more valuable when it can be sustained across units and adapted responsibly across programmes.

Six questions for programme leaders

01 | ScopeDo we know which industrial capabilities are required, by whom, and at what level of assurance?
02 | TimingWill supplier qualifications and workforce capability mature before they become critical-path dependencies?
03 | IntegrationWho owns the interfaces between design, procurement, manufacturing, testing and construction?
04 | EvidenceCan suppliers demonstrate conformity through records and objective evidence acceptable for their assigned scope?
05 | LearningHow will knowledge and qualified competence remain available after the first contract or unit?
06 | ScaleWhich capabilities can realistically support subsequent units, future projects or export markets?

These questions matter for Poland's emerging nuclear industry, for established European supply chains supporting multiple programmes and for countries considering SMRs as a route into nuclear power. The answers will differ. The strategic requirement to turn participation into sustainable delivery capability will not.

Closing perspective

Nuclear specialists remain indispensable. Nuclear safety, regulation, licensing, specialist engineering and nuclear quality cannot be replaced by general industrial experience.

But a national nuclear programme cannot be delivered by nuclear specialists alone. It needs the construction sector, manufacturing industry, laboratories, logistics, engineering services and many other disciplines to contribute within a coherent and controlled delivery system.

The objective should not be to prepare industry for a single nuclear contract. It should be to build an industrial base capable of delivering nuclear work safely, predictably and repeatedly.

That is when local participation becomes industrial capability, and industrial capability becomes a long-term strategic asset.
