Nuclear Programme Strategy & Industrial Capability

Nuclear Plants Will Not Be Built by Nuclear Experts Alone

Building industrial capability that lasts beyond the first nuclear project.

By Ievgen Petrenko | Published 9 October 2026

The first nuclear power project demands the creation of a new delivery environment. A country must develop its nuclear infrastructure, establish capable organisations, secure technology, prepare for construction and maintain a clear basis for safety and regulatory oversight. But the challenge changes when one project becomes a long-term programme.

Building the first plant is a project milestone. Being able to deliver the next plant without rebuilding the industrial system from the beginning is a programme capability.

Nuclear expertise is essential. It is not enough.

Nuclear safety, licensing, regulatory affairs, engineering and nuclear quality remain essential. Yet delivery depends just as heavily on civil contractors, manufacturers, design consultancies, laboratories, logistics companies, procurement specialists and project controls teams. A successful programme needs all of them to work within a controlled nuclear delivery environment.

Most of these organisations do not need to become nuclear specialists across their entire business. They need to understand the requirements applicable to their scope, how those requirements change everyday decisions and what evidence is needed to demonstrate conformity.

A strong industrial base is therefore a starting advantage, not proof of nuclear readiness. Experience on complex infrastructure projects helps, but it does not automatically provide the necessary configuration control, traceability, qualified procedures, supplier oversight or disciplined management of interfaces.

The objective is not to make every industrial company a nuclear company. It is to make every participating company capable of delivering its assigned scope reliably, with controls proportionate to its safety significance.

Industrial readiness needs programme-level accountability

Supplier development cannot be left entirely to isolated procurement packages. A contractor may fulfil one contract successfully and still leave little lasting capability behind if the trained workforce disperses, qualified processes are abandoned or key lessons never reach the next project.

This risk grows when decisions on project sequencing, localisation, procurement strategy and workforce development are made separately. Delays between major work packages can erode hard-won experience. The next unit may then require renewed training, supplier assessments and investment in systems that the first unit already helped to establish.

No single organisation can own the whole industrial ecosystem. But a nuclear programme needs identifiable responsibility for coordinating capability-development plans, monitoring delivery gaps and preserving knowledge across successive projects. This does not remove the contractual responsibilities of owners, EPC organisations or suppliers; it makes their longer-term interfaces visible.

Four capabilities must develop together

Industrial readiness is more than a collection of supplier certificates. It depends on four mutually reinforcing capabilities:

  • Capability

    Competent people, facilities, resources and work processes suitable for the assigned scope.

  • Integration

    Reliable engineering, procurement, construction, testing and configuration interfaces.

  • Assurance

    Objective evidence that applicable technical, safety and quality requirements have been met.

  • Continuity

    Retention of skills, qualified processes and lessons for later units and future work.

A competent workforce without an effective delivery system cannot reliably perform. An accredited laboratory without the correct technical scope does not solve an inspection problem. A capable manufacturer with unclear design interfaces may create rework even when its own product meets specification. These are programme integration issues, not isolated supplier shortcomings.

Clear roles matter more than blurred responsibilities

Governments shape long-term policy and the enabling environment. Owners and future operators must develop the capability to define, govern, accept and eventually operate the plant. Technology providers and EPC organisations bring design and delivery expertise. Engineering and industrial suppliers perform their contractual scopes and demonstrate conformity with the applicable requirements.

Independent regulators have a distinct statutory role in establishing or applying the regulatory framework and conducting oversight. Their independence is not an instrument of localisation policy, delivery acceleration or commercial negotiation. Strong coordination across industry should strengthen, not weaken, the integrity of nuclear safety decisions.

Competence can travel. Qualification does not always travel with it.

The experience gained on one unit can be valuable for the next. Familiarity with disciplined construction, safety culture, traceable records, inspection and supplier management can often be retained and adapted. But competence is not the same as a project-specific qualification.

A supplier qualified for one technology, scope or customer cannot assume automatic acceptance on another. Different plant designs, codes, jurisdictions, contractual requirements and safety classifications may require additional assessments, procedures, testing or evidence. The aim is to reuse what remains valid without carrying across assumptions that have not been verified.

This distinction is especially important where national plans envisage multiple large reactors, SMRs or more than one technology. A broader industrial base can create strategic flexibility, but only if transferability is assessed systematically rather than presumed.

Localisation must survive the first contract

Local spending is visible. Durable industrial capability is harder to measure. Localisation has strategic value when firms retain the ability to deliver increasingly demanding work, support maintenance during plant operation and compete for future contracts in other projects or markets.

A programme that maximises domestic work in the first unit but neglects continuity may find itself buying back the same capability later. A programme that plans the transition between units can convert the learning curve of the first project into better productivity, stronger supplier relationships and lower exposure to avoidable requalification and interface risk.

The IAEA's Milestones Approach, including its 2024 second revision and annex on small modular reactors, provides an internationally recognised framework for considering the infrastructure that supports a nuclear power programme. Industrial involvement is one part of that broader picture, alongside issues such as human resources, management, procurement, safety and regulation.

Measure delivery capability, not only participation

Local contract value, the number of registered suppliers and training attendance are useful inputs. They should not be mistaken for proof of readiness. Programme leaders need evidence of whether capability is improving between contracts and whether critical delivery risks are being reduced.

  • Workforce and organisational continuity

    Retention of competent personnel, maintained qualifications where applicable, up-to-date procedures and demonstrable knowledge transfer.

  • Assurance and document quality

    Timely availability of traceable records, first-pass acceptance of deliverables, and effective closure of quality findings.

  • Interface performance

    Time to resolve significant design, procurement and construction interfaces, including ageing of critical open decisions.

  • Repeatability and resilience

    Proven performance across successive packages, suitably qualified alternative capacity where needed, and readiness for future work.

These indicators need context. More reported nonconformances may initially reflect better detection and a stronger reporting culture, not deteriorating performance. The quality of evaluation, corrective action and timely closure is more informative than an isolated count. Similarly, a low number of reported interface issues may simply mean that problems are not being surfaced.

No industrial readiness metric overrides compliance with licensing conditions, technical specifications or nuclear safety requirements. The measures are intended to inform programme planning, supplier development and governance decisions.

The strategic choice

The new nuclear fleet will not be built by the nuclear industry alone. It will be built with the wider industrial base, including companies that are entering nuclear delivery for the first time.

The decision facing programme leaders is whether those companies are prepared only for a single work package, or whether their skills, systems and experience become a capability that the country can retain, improve and use again.

Projects deliver plants. Programmes must also develop the industrial capacity to deliver repeatedly.
