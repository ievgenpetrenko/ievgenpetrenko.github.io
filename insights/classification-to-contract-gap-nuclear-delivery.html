The Classification-to-Contract Gap in Nuclear Delivery

Four Decision Gates for Maintaining Requirements Integrity Across International Nuclear Supply Chains

By Ievgen Petrenko | Published 22 October 2026 | Independent Professional Insight

Executive Decision Summary

Europe's expanding nuclear supply chains face a challenge that cannot be resolved through supplier certification or individual quality audits alone.

A nuclear project may have correctly classified equipment, established engineering requirements, qualified suppliers and approved QA procedures, yet still fail to maintain complete requirements continuity across organizational and contractual interfaces.

This article identifies that risk as the Classification-to-Contract Gap.

It proposes a Classification-to-Evidence Integrity (CEI) model, a cross-functional decision-assurance approach designed to complement existing engineering, procurement, configuration management and nuclear QA arrangements.

The model introduces four decision gates:

  1. Requirements Baseline: Are the requirements sufficiently mature and controlled to support the proposed procurement commitment?
  2. Supplier Capability and Contractual Readiness: Can the supplier demonstrate the ability to perform the defined work under the applicable requirements?
  3. Manufacturing or Work Authorization: Have the necessary prerequisites been satisfied before the affected activities begin?
  4. Acceptance Evidence: Does the completed work have sufficient objective evidence to support the required acceptance decision?

The objective is not to introduce another nuclear standard or approval system. It is to make existing responsibilities, unresolved interfaces and evidence requirements visible before they become manufacturing, procurement or acceptance problems.

The practical value of CEI lies in testing the integrity of decisions between organizations, not duplicating the controls already established within them.

1. From Audit Integration to Decision Integrity

A nuclear programme can operate several effective management systems and still experience difficulties at the interfaces between participating organizations.

Safety classification establishes the significance of structures, systems and components (SSCs) within the applicable regulatory and design framework. Engineering translates safety functions into technical requirements, specifications and acceptance criteria. Procurement incorporates relevant obligations into contracts and purchase orders. Suppliers implement these requirements through controlled engineering, manufacturing, inspection, testing and supporting activities. QA provides systematic assurance that applicable requirements have been correctly implemented.

Each discipline has defined responsibilities and established controls. However, effective performance within individual functions does not necessarily demonstrate that their interfaces are equally effective.

A technical change may be properly approved but not communicated to every affected supplier. A procurement package may contain an approved specification but omit a necessary inspection requirement. A manufacturer may produce complete quality records against a superseded revision of a technical document. An audit may correctly conclude that a supplier has followed its approved procedures, while the project's actual acceptance requirements remain incompletely addressed.

The problem is not necessarily the absence of procedures. It may be the loss of requirements integrity between procedures, organizations and decisions.

In my previous Insight, Two Audit Frameworks. One Nuclear Project. Who Owns the Gaps?, I examined why different nuclear audit frameworks can reach legitimate but distinct conclusions while project-level assurance gaps remain unresolved.

That analysis focused on the integration of assurance. This article addresses the next question:

How can a nuclear programme demonstrate that the conditions necessary for successful execution and acceptance have been established before the corresponding decisions are made?

This shifts the discussion from audit coordination toward decision integrity across the nuclear project lifecycle.

2. The Classification-to-Contract Gap

Safety classification and supplier qualification are connected, but they are not interchangeable decisions.

A safety classification establishes the significance of an SSC according to the applicable design and regulatory requirements. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that every associated engineering, procurement, qualification, manufacturing, inspection and documentation obligation has been correctly established and communicated.

Consider a safety-classified component. The design authority may have correctly determined its safety function. Engineering may have developed the technical specification. Procurement may have issued an approved purchase order. The supplier may possess a qualified manufacturing process.

Nevertheless, a required verification activity may be missing from the inspection and test plan. Alternatively, a revision affecting acceptance criteria may not have reached a lower-tier supplier before manufacturing begins.

The classification may be correct. The individual management processes may be functioning. Yet the relationship between the applicable requirement and the actual work is incomplete.

The Classification-to-Contract Gap is the potential loss of completeness, consistency, timing, traceability or decision accountability when requirements derived from nuclear safety and engineering decisions are transferred into contractual and execution obligations.

This is an analytical concept, not a new regulatory classification. It describes a possible weakness at the intersection of existing project processes.

Two outcomes are particularly undesirable:

  • Under-application of requirements may lead to insufficient verification, missing evidence, unacceptable work or the need for additional technical justification and rework.
  • Over-application of requirements may introduce unnecessary controls, duplicated qualification activities, avoidable costs and additional barriers to industrial participation.

Both can undermine the efficiency of supplier qualification and project delivery.

The objective should therefore be neither minimum compliance nor maximum procedural complexity. The objective is justified, complete and demonstrable application of the requirements relevant to the actual work.

3. Why the Challenge Extends Across National Regulatory Frameworks

European nuclear programmes increasingly involve reactor technologies, engineering organizations and industrial suppliers originating from different countries.

Large NPPs and SMRs based on technologies developed by Westinghouse, GE Vernova Hitachi, Holtec and Canadian reactor technology providers may involve several national regulatory frameworks and international QA standards. The underlying nuclear safety objectives may be similar. The regulatory definitions and legal obligations are not automatically equivalent.

Different concepts of nuclear safety significance

Under U.S. 10 CFR 50.2, safety-related is formally defined in relation to SSCs performing specified safety functions. Safety-related SSCs are generally understood as a subset of the broader U.S. concept of important to safety.

ISO 19443 uses another term: important to nuclear safety (ITNS). It addresses products, services, items and activities whose failure could result in undue radiation exposure of people or the environment.

National European legislation establishes its own requirements for safety classification. These concepts share nuclear safety objectives, but their definitions, origins and applicable obligations differ. They should not be treated as automatically interchangeable.

Poland and the Czech Republic as examples

Poland and the Czech Republic illustrate the problem. They are examples, not representatives of the full diversity of European nuclear regulatory systems.

Poland. Article 36j of the Polish Atomic Law establishes requirements for safety classification of relevant nuclear facility systems and elements of structures and equipment, including applicable instrumentation and control software. Classification takes account of safety functions, consequences of failure, possible initiating events and the likelihood that the equipment will be required to perform its safety function.

Article 36k separately addresses integrated management systems and appropriate quality-system requirements for contractors and suppliers within its scope. Safety classification and quality assurance obligations are therefore connected but remain distinct requirements.

The Czech Republic. Act No. 263/2016 Coll., the Atomic Act, and its implementing regulations establish requirements concerning selected equipment, safety functions, national safety classes, quality assurance and technical conformity.

The Czech framework distinguishes selected equipment performing nuclear safety functions from other equipment that may affect nuclear safety. Consequently, the absence of a directly credited safety function does not necessarily eliminate the need to consider potential effects on safety-classified equipment.

Neither the Polish nor the Czech framework can be translated automatically into a U.S. safety-related classification. IAEA SSG-30 provides an internationally recognized methodology for safety classification. However, methodological alignment does not establish legal equivalence between national classification systems.

National safety classifications must retain their regulatory meaning while the project establishes the associated engineering and QA requirements.

ASME NQA-1, ISO 19443 and Canadian frameworks

ASME NQA-1 is primarily associated with nuclear quality assurance for safety-related SSCs and activities affecting their safety-related functions, particularly in U.S.-origin nuclear programmes.

Under 10 CFR 50 Appendix B, relevant QA requirements extend to activities affecting those functions, including engineering, procurement, manufacturing, inspection, testing and other applicable services.

However, the potential scope of NQA-1 as a nuclear QA standard is broader than its particular regulatory application under Appendix B. Its application may also be established through licensing, organizational or contractual arrangements.

ISO 19443 provides a nuclear supply-chain QMS framework for organizations supplying ITNS products and services. ISO 19443 certification is valuable evidence of an assessed management system within its certified scope, but it does not automatically establish compliance with every project-specific NQA-1 requirement.

Canadian nuclear programmes use CSA N286 for nuclear facility management systems and the CSA N299 series for supplier QA requirements. NQA-1 may also apply where specified by the relevant project arrangements.

Canadian nuclear technology and Canadian nuclear supply-chain experience are not identical concepts. CANDU is Canadian-origin reactor technology. BWRX-300 is U.S.-origin reactor technology being deployed in Canada and pursued for European applications.

Experience developed by Canadian suppliers supporting BWRX-300 may be valuable for European projects, but that does not automatically transfer Canadian regulatory approvals or qualify the supplier under another country's requirements.

These differences illustrate why reactor technology, national classification, QA applicability and supplier qualification must be assessed separately.

4. Why This Matters in October 2026

Two developments illustrate the practical significance of staged supplier qualification and international industrial capability.

Poland: conditional AP1000 supplier qualification

On 8 October 2026, Polskie Elektrownie Jądrowe commissioned Westinghouse to begin the long-lead item procurement process for the first AP1000 unit at Lubiatowo-Kopalino. The announced scope includes preparation of technical documentation, quality and oversight plans, supplier selection and contracting. Production and delivery of completed components are expected under a subsequent order.

On 13 October 2026, Westinghouse announced conditional purchase orders for safety-related module scopes involving four Polish companies:

  • Energomontaż-Północ Gdynia: CA03 module.
  • FAMAK: CA20 floors module.
  • Mostostal Kielce: CA02 walls module.
  • Mostostal Krakow: CA05 walls module.

According to Westinghouse, the award of the full project scopes is conditional upon NQA-1 certification and successful fabrication of pre-production qualification mockups.

These developments illustrate that industrial capability, contractual qualification conditions and authorization for full production are distinct considerations. A successful qualification mockup does not automatically establish conformity of every subsequently manufactured module. Nor does a conditional purchase order mean that all later technical and acceptance decisions have already been completed. Those decisions remain subject to the actual project requirements and authorized processes.

The broader lesson is that supplier readiness must be demonstrated progressively, with clear conditions governing advancement to subsequent activities.

The announced arrangements are not presented here as an implementation of CEI. They provide a relevant example of staged supplier development.

Canada and Europe: BWRX-300 industrial capability

On 17 June 2026, GE Vernova Hitachi Nuclear Energy and Canadian manufacturer Velan announced a collaboration to explore opportunities for supplying Reactor Integral Isolation Valves and Containment Isolation Valves to European BWRX-300 projects. The collaboration builds on experience developed through the Darlington New Nuclear Project in Ontario.

Industrial capability demonstrated in one nuclear programme may support future opportunities elsewhere. However, reuse of technical experience does not automatically establish compliance with another project's regulatory, engineering, procurement and acceptance requirements. The supplier must demonstrate which qualifications, controls and evidence remain applicable and which require additional verification.

The objective is portability of demonstrated capability, not automatic portability of regulatory classifications, contractual obligations or approvals.

This principle is relevant to both large NPPs and SMRs, particularly when supply chains extend across jurisdictions.

5. Three Levels of Supplier Readiness

Supplier readiness should not be reduced to a single certification or approval status.

Level 1. Organizational Readiness

Does the supplier possess the management arrangements, personnel, facilities, technical qualifications and resources necessary to perform a defined category of nuclear work?

An ISO 19443 certificate may provide relevant evidence within its scope. Additional capabilities may be required for particular nuclear applications. This readiness level can be assessed before a specific contract is awarded.

Level 2. Contractual Readiness

Can the supplier demonstrate that it understands and can implement the actual technical, QA, inspection, documentation and acceptance requirements of a defined procurement package?

Organizational capability alone does not establish contractual readiness. A manufacturer may have qualified welding procedures, competent personnel and effective inspection controls but still need additional arrangements for a specific nuclear contract.

Level 3. Delivery Readiness

Has the defined product, service or work package been completed and verified in accordance with the applicable requirements? Are the required records available? Have relevant nonconformities and deviations received necessary authorized dispositions? Can the responsible organization make the required acceptance decision?

A contractually qualified supplier may still have an incomplete or unacceptable delivery package.

Organizational readiness supports supplier development. Contractual readiness supports controlled execution. Delivery readiness supports acceptance.

These levels provide a basis for assessing progress without confusing capability, qualification and final conformity. They also form the operational foundation for the CEI model.

6. The Classification-to-Evidence Integrity Model

The proposed Classification-to-Evidence Integrity (CEI) model is a cross-functional decision-assurance approach. It examines whether requirements remain complete, controlled and verifiable across organizational and contractual interfaces.

Dimension 1. Requirements Integrity

The first dimension establishes the continuity of requirements across seven connected elements:

Safety Function → National Classification → Engineering Requirements → QA Applicability → Supplier Capability → Controlled Execution → Acceptance Evidence

This is a logical model, not a mandatory chronological sequence. Some activities occur in parallel, and classification or engineering requirements may evolve as the design matures. The purpose is to ensure that those relationships remain controlled.

Dimension 2. Decision Accountability

The second dimension identifies the organizations authorized to establish, communicate, implement, verify and accept requirements.

Each significant requirement should have:

  • An identifiable source and applicable revision.
  • A defined scope and applicability basis.
  • An authorized decision owner.
  • An implementing organization.
  • A required verification method.
  • An acceptance authority.
  • A mechanism for controlling subsequent changes.

The licence holder retains statutory nuclear safety responsibilities under the applicable national framework. Design authorities, engineering organizations, procurement functions, QA organizations and suppliers retain their respective authorized responsibilities.

Contractual allocation of work does not transfer the licence holder's non-delegable responsibility for nuclear safety.

CEI does not replace these responsibilities. It examines whether the interfaces connecting them are complete and effective.

What does CEI add to established nuclear QA?

NQA-1, ISO 19443, applicable CSA standards and project procedures already address many relevant engineering, procurement and quality controls. CEI does not claim to introduce those requirements.

Instead, it proposes a method for testing four questions across organizational boundaries:

  • Requirement continuity: Has a significant requirement been correctly translated from its authorized source into all relevant downstream obligations?
  • Decision ownership: Is there a clearly identifiable organization authorized to resolve the requirement or approve the next activity?
  • Readiness at the point of commitment: Were the necessary prerequisites satisfied when a procurement, manufacturing or acceptance decision was made?
  • Evidence continuity: Can the final conformity evidence be traced to the applicable requirement and controlled configuration?

These questions provide a common assessment structure for identifying gaps that may not be visible when functions are evaluated independently.

The added value of CEI is not another set of QA requirements. It is an integrated test of whether existing requirements and decision processes work together.

7. Four CEI Decision Gates

The model proposes four decision gates that can be integrated into existing project governance arrangements. They are not additional statutory licensing stages, new regulatory hold points or substitutes for established technical approvals.

Each gate examines whether a defined decision is supported by an adequate requirements baseline, responsible authority and objective evidence.

Gate 1. Requirements Baseline

Decision question: Is the defined scope sufficiently mature and controlled to support the proposed procurement commitment?

The relevant engineering, procurement and QA functions should establish the requirements applicable to the intended product, service or activity. These may include safety classification, technical codes, design specifications, QA requirements, inspection obligations and acceptance criteria. The assessment must distinguish approved information from controlled provisional information and unresolved decisions.

  • GO: The required baseline has been established to the maturity necessary for the proposed procurement commitment, and relevant prerequisites are satisfied.
  • CONDITIONAL GO: The decision may proceed within explicitly defined restrictions, provided unresolved matters do not constitute mandatory prerequisites for that specific commitment. For example, limited procurement-planning or capacity-reservation activity may proceed while a final manufacturing specification remains under controlled development, if governing arrangements permit it.
  • NO-GO: An essential requirement or required authorization is absent, inconsistent or unresolved in a way that prevents the proposed commitment.

Decision ownership: Authorized engineering and procurement functions, with required participation of the design authority, licence holder and QA organization.

Required output: Controlled requirements baseline, unresolved-interface register and documented procurement decision.

Gate 2. Supplier Capability and Contractual Readiness

Decision question: Can this supplier perform the defined work under the applicable requirements?

The assessment establishes whether existing supplier capabilities satisfy the relevant contractual and nuclear QA requirements. For an ISO 19443-certified manufacturer, this may include an NQA-1 gap assessment where NQA-1 is applicable. Existing compliant processes should be recognized rather than duplicated unnecessarily. Actual deficiencies should be distinguished from requirements unresolved within the customer's organization.

  • GO: The supplier has demonstrated capabilities required for the defined scope and satisfied applicable qualification conditions.
  • CONDITIONAL GO: The supplier may be approved for limited scope or specified preparatory activities while identified deficiencies are closed, where permitted. Engineering preparation or qualification mockup activities may be authorized without release for full safety-related production.
  • NO-GO: The supplier cannot demonstrate a mandatory capability, required qualification or compliance condition necessary for the proposed work.

Decision ownership: Authorized procurement and supplier qualification functions, supported by engineering and QA.

Required output: Scope-specific supplier readiness decision, including open conditions and responsible owners.

Gate 3. Manufacturing or Work Authorization

Decision question: Have the necessary prerequisites been satisfied before the affected work begins?

This gate examines the actual work package. It considers controlled design documentation, manufacturing instructions, technical qualifications, inspection and test plans, material requirements and applicable hold points. It also verifies that relevant design changes have been incorporated.

  • GO: Required work documentation, qualifications, verification arrangements and authorizations are available and applicable.
  • CONDITIONAL GO: Only a specifically authorized portion of work may proceed, with restrictions recorded and controlled. For example, preparatory activities may continue while release of an affected fabrication step remains prohibited.
  • NO-GO: A mandatory technical, qualification, inspection or authorization prerequisite remains unsatisfied.

Decision ownership: Authorized work-release function, with necessary engineering, manufacturing or construction, QA and customer interfaces.

Required output: Documented work-release decision identifying authorized scope, applicable configuration and restrictions.

Gate 4. Acceptance Evidence

Decision question: Does the completed work have sufficient objective evidence to support the required acceptance decision?

Physical completion does not automatically establish conformity. The acceptance package must demonstrate compliance with applicable specifications, revisions, inspection and testing requirements, and approved dispositions.

  • GO: All requirements necessary for the defined acceptance decision have been satisfied and supported by adequate objective evidence.
  • CONDITIONAL GO: A limited or conditional acceptance may be made only where regulatory, technical and contractual arrangements expressly permit it. An intermediate acceptance milestone may be completed while documentation remains due before a separate final release, where permitted.
  • NO-GO: Required conformity evidence is absent, unresolved nonconformities lack authorized disposition, or an acceptance prerequisite has not been satisfied.

Decision ownership: Designated technical and contractual acceptance authorities.

Required output: Acceptance decision linked to applicable configuration, verification evidence and outstanding conditions.

Conditional GO must never become a mechanism for bypassing mandatory regulatory, technical or contractual requirements. Where a required hold point, qualification or approval has not been satisfied, affected work cannot proceed merely because schedule pressure makes continuation desirable. The decision gates must reinforce existing authority, not weaken it.

8. How CEI Would Work in Practice

Consider a hypothetical European manufacturer producing welded steel assemblies for a nuclear project. The company operates an ISO 19443-certified management system and has established welding, material traceability and inspection processes. It receives a procurement specification and associated inspection and test plan.

During preparation for manufacturing, engineering approves a design modification affecting one welded attachment. The modification introduces revised verification requirements. Engineering correctly approves the change, but the revised requirement is not fully incorporated into supplier manufacturing documentation.

The manufacturer performs work using the previous inspection plan. The records appear complete against the documents available to the workshop. Nevertheless, the evidence may not establish conformity with the latest approved design requirements.

This is an interface failure. It does not automatically demonstrate that the original safety classification was incorrect or the supplier's entire QMS ineffective.

Gate Verification question Potential gap
Requirements Baseline Was the design revision assessed against affected technical and procurement obligations? Incomplete change impact assessment
Supplier Readiness Were revised obligations transmitted to the supplier and relevant lower tiers? Incomplete requirements flow-down
Work Authorization Were work instructions and the ITP updated before affected fabrication? Work released against an outdated baseline
Acceptance Evidence Can actual manufactured configuration be verified against current approved requirements? Incomplete conformity evidence

The value of the assessment is that it identifies the location of the failed interface. The response may require engineering evaluation, document revision, additional inspection, authorized disposition or corrective action. The specific action must be determined under applicable technical and QA processes.

CEI does not independently authorize disposition of nonconforming items. Its role is to make the relationship between requirements, responsibilities and evidence visible.

9. Four Recurring Interface Failure Mechanisms

The proposed model identifies four recurring categories of interface risk.

1. Classification without complete requirements allocation. Safety significance is established, but relevant engineering, procurement or verification obligations are not fully communicated.

2. Requirements introduced after capability decisions. Supplier selection or manufacturing preparation proceeds before significant technical or QA obligations have been sufficiently defined.

3. Evidence disconnected from requirements. Manufacturing, inspection or testing records exist but cannot demonstrate conformity with applicable requirement, revision or configuration.

4. Changes that do not propagate. An authorized decision does not reach all affected organizations, documents and activities.

These mechanisms may arise in different organizational environments. The important distinction is between an actual nonconformity against an applicable requirement, a supplier capability gap, an unresolved engineering or classification decision, and an incomplete customer or procurement interface. These categories must not be merged into a generic supplier nonconformity register.

A finding is useful only when the responsible organization has the authority and means to resolve its underlying cause.

10. The Minimum CEI Decision Record

A practical CEI implementation should not require another comprehensive project database. It can use existing requirements management, engineering document control, procurement and QA systems.

Information field Purpose
Requirement identifier Establishes the requirement being controlled
Requirement source and revision Identifies governing technical, regulatory or contractual baseline
Safety function and classification reference Identifies applicable safety significance and source documentation
QA applicability Identifies the programme, standard, edition and provisions
Responsible decision authority Establishes who may approve or resolve the issue
Implementing organization Identifies who must execute the requirement
Contract and work-package reference Links requirement to actual work
Verification and acceptance evidence Identifies how conformity will be demonstrated
Decision status GO, CONDITIONAL GO or NO-GO, where applicable
Outstanding conditions and restrictions Prevents unresolved matters from being treated as closed
Change status Identifies subsequent changes affecting the decision

The record should be proportionate to the significance and complexity of work. Every individual manufacturing record need not reference the original safety analysis directly. Required relationships should be demonstrable through the controlled documentation system.

Bidirectional verification

Forward traceability: Can a significant requirement be followed from authorized source through procurement and execution to objective evidence demonstrating conformity?

Backward traceability: Can a selected inspection, test or acceptance record be traced to governing requirement, specification, revision, configuration and authorized acceptance basis?

Both tests should use samples appropriate to risk and scope. Their purpose is to identify missing connections, not generate additional documentation without technical value.

11. How Could CEI Be Validated?

A methodology should not be judged only by the quality of its conceptual description. Its usefulness should be tested against actual nuclear procurement and supplier management processes.

An initial controlled pilot could use 10 to 20 representative procurement or work packages, selected for differences in safety significance, scope, technical complexity and requirements maturity. This is an illustrative pilot size, not a validated sampling standard.

  1. Establish existing controls. Identify current arrangements for requirements allocation, supplier qualification, manufacturing release, change control and acceptance. Recognize effective processes rather than assuming deficiencies.
  2. Perform cross-functional interface tests. Sample requirements and decisions connecting engineering, procurement, supplier QA and acceptance. Record allocation, ownership, traceability and change-propagation gaps.
  3. Classify findings correctly. Separate nonconformities from capability gaps, unresolved technical decisions and interface clarification issues. Assign the organization empowered to resolve each.
  4. Test gate effectiveness. Assess whether the CEI gates identify significant open matters before the next affected activity or commitment.
  5. Assess outcomes. Compare findings with those that existing controls would already have identified.

Potential pilot indicators include unresolved requirements identified before release, missing lower-tier flow-down, decisions without clear authority, incomplete traceability, late changes and acceptance delays due to deficient evidence.

A useful CEI implementation should demonstrate additional diagnostic value without unnecessarily duplicating established controls. An initial pilot would test operational usefulness, not prove cost savings, schedule gains or safety improvements. Such claims require further evidence.

12. Relevance to Nuclear Programme Stakeholders

Licence holders and programme owners

CEI can support oversight of interfaces between engineering, procurement, suppliers and acceptance. It does not transfer nuclear safety responsibility away from the licence holder; it improves visibility of how requirements and decisions move through the supply chain.

Reactor technology providers and design authorities

The method can help identify requirements that must be preserved when design information becomes specifications, procurement packages or manufacturing instructions, including consequences of later changes.

EPC organizations

CEI can provide a common diagnostic structure for engineering, procurement, construction-readiness and QA interfaces, especially where different organizations control successive stages of a requirement.

Nuclear regulators

CEI does not establish a new classification or approval mechanism. Its potential relevance lies in demonstrating the implementation of established requirements and assigned responsibilities in the applicable national framework.

Industrial suppliers

The approach helps distinguish reusable capabilities from project-specific obligations and clarifies which problems the supplier must resolve versus those requiring a customer or design authority decision.

The common objective is greater requirements transparency without weakening established responsibilities.

13. Large NPPs and SMRs: Different Industrial Models, Shared Requirements Risks

Large NPP programmes involve extensive engineering interfaces, complex procurement structures, multiple subcontracting levels and substantial manufacturing and construction documentation.

SMR programmes emphasize standardization, modular manufacturing, production repeatability and potentially broader international supplier participation. These different delivery models share a requirements challenge.

A manufacturing process can be standardized. A technical qualification may support several applications. A supplier may maintain one integrated QMS across projects. Yet applicable national classifications, licensing commitments, technical specifications and contractual requirements must still be established for each intended scope.

Standardization should enable controlled reuse of demonstrated capabilities. It should not be used to assume equivalence between different regulatory or contractual requirements.

For large NPPs, CEI may help assess complex interfaces between engineering, procurement and construction. For SMRs, it may help establish what requirements and evidence can legitimately be reused across repeated manufacturing activities, modules and projects.

The proposed method supports requirements continuity as technical work moves between organizations.

14. What CEI Does Not Claim

CEI does not introduce new nuclear safety requirements. It does not replace ASME NQA-1, ISO 19443, CSA standards, national nuclear regulations or applicable technical codes. It does not independently determine national safety classification. It does not substitute for approved engineering decisions, licensing obligations or customer acceptance authority.

It does not provide universal supplier qualification or imply that existing nuclear QA systems lack design control, procurement control, configuration management or auditing. Those controls are already established.

CEI instead proposes an integrated way to examine whether they are effectively connected across organizational and contractual boundaries. Its potential value is diagnostic and managerial and must be demonstrated through practical application.

A methodology is useful not because it introduces new terminology, but because it helps organizations identify and resolve problems existing arrangements may otherwise leave insufficiently visible.

Conclusion: Requirements Integrity Is a Delivery Responsibility

European nuclear programmes do not need to make national safety classifications, ISO 19443, ASME NQA-1 and Canadian CSA frameworks artificially equivalent. Each has a legitimate role within its applicable regulatory, technical and contractual context.

The challenge is connecting them through coherent engineering, procurement, supplier qualification and delivery arrangements.

The Classification-to-Contract Gap describes the potential loss of requirements integrity between these arrangements. The proposed CEI model provides a cross-functional approach through Requirements Integrity and Decision Accountability, supported by four governance gates.

For European large NPP and SMR programmes using international reactor technologies, the objective is practical: ensure significant requirements remain controlled from authorized source through each relevant interface; ensure responsible organizations understand obligations; ensure critical decisions are supported by evidence; and identify open requirements before they become execution or acceptance problems.

The maturity of a nuclear programme is not demonstrated solely by the number of requirements established, procedures approved or audits completed. It is also demonstrated by the integrity of the decisions connecting those requirements to actual work and verifiable conformity.

Nuclear safety requirements do not end when classification is approved. Their effectiveness depends on whether they remain intact from design decisions to final acceptance.

Methodological Note

The Classification-to-Contract Gap and CEI model are proposed analytical and management concepts developed in this Insight. They are not established regulatory classifications, independent nuclear safety standards or regulator-endorsed supplier qualification methodologies.

The gates support existing project governance arrangements and do not replace statutory approvals, mandatory hold points, authorized engineering decisions, contractual acceptance requirements or independent nuclear QA oversight.

The model has not yet been validated through a documented project pilot; measurable performance claims require such validation. Applicable requirements must always be established from governing legislation, licensing and design basis, contractual obligations and approved technical documentation.
