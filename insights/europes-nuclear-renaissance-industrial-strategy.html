Nuclear Programme Strategy & Industrial Capability

Europe's Nuclear Renaissance Needs an Industrial Strategy

Europe can announce new reactors, but only an integrated industrial system can deliver them safely, competitively and repeatedly.

By Ievgen Petrenko | 9 October 2026 | Approx. 12 min read | Expands a LinkedIn article published on 20 July 2026

Europe's nuclear ambitions are becoming larger and more diverse. Existing reactor fleets need sustained support and lifetime-extension investment. New large-scale projects are moving through national programmes. Small modular reactors are being considered for electricity, heat and industrial applications.

The European Commission's March 2026 Nuclear Illustrative Programme estimates approximately €241 billion in present-value investment needs by 2050 for the lifetime extension of existing reactors and new large-scale nuclear projects in its base-case scenario. This figure does not include all future expenditure on SMRs, other emerging technologies, decommissioning or radioactive-waste management. Its SMR strategy places supply chains, industrial cooperation, regulatory cooperation and skilled people at the centre of deployment.

Those policy directions matter. But investment ambition and industrial delivery capability are not the same thing. The estimate is a scale indicator, not a funded procurement pipeline or a forecast that every project will proceed.

The key strategic question is not only how many reactors Europe intends to build. It is whether Europe is building the industrial system capable of delivering and sustaining them.

From energy policy to industrial execution

A nuclear programme may select a technology, identify sites and establish financing. Yet the delivery system must bring together an owner, regulator, designers, vendors, EPC contractors, manufacturers, qualified suppliers, laboratories, construction companies and future operators.

Each organisation has distinct responsibilities. Engineering decisions affect licensing. Licensing requirements affect procurement. Procurement specifications affect manufacturing. Manufacturing and construction evidence determine what can be accepted, commissioned and handed over for operation.

Industrial policy must therefore develop integration capability, not merely production capacity. More suppliers create opportunity, but also more interfaces, more cross-border dependencies and more requirements that must remain controlled.

Local content is not the final objective

National industrial participation is a legitimate goal. It can create skilled jobs, strengthen domestic resilience and give companies a place in international nuclear supply chains. But a local-content percentage does not, by itself, demonstrate that a supplier is qualified for its assigned scope or capable of reliable delivery.

Industrial strategy should begin with an evidence-based capability map: what companies can already deliver, what additional controls they need, where investment is justified, and which capabilities are better secured through trusted international partnerships.

The intended outcome should be competitive and repeatable nuclear delivery capability, capable of supporting more than one unit, one customer or one national project. A supplier's share of spending is an input measure; the ability to deliver accepted work safely and consistently is an outcome.

Eight pillars of a nuclear industrial strategy

In my July 2026 article on industrial policy for the nuclear renaissance, I proposed eight complementary pillars. Their programme-level significance is worth setting out more sharply in a European context.

PILLAR 01

Long-term national commitment

Credible project pipelines, stable governance, procurement visibility and predictable decisions make long-cycle industrial investment possible.

PILLAR 02

Competitive manufacturing capability

Strengthen realistic industrial strengths in mechanical, electrical, I&C, testing, modular and advanced manufacturing, without assuming self-sufficiency.

PILLAR 03

Strong nuclear supply chains

Qualify suppliers for defined scopes, develop capable SMEs and create routes into international programmes beyond the domestic pipeline.

PILLAR 04

Workforce and knowledge

Develop the right skills at the right programme phase and retain experience beyond temporary teams, first units and individual contracts.

PILLAR 05

Nuclear quality infrastructure

Build practical assurance capability: suitable management systems, oversight, traceability, inspection, laboratories and objective evidence.

PILLAR 06

Common terms and clear rules

Make requirements understandable across contracts and jurisdictions, especially safety classification, design authority and procurement flow-down.

PILLAR 07

Transfer of delivery capability

Transfer engineering methods, regulatory knowledge, project controls, configuration management and supplier oversight, not merely technical designs.

PILLAR 08

International cooperation

Combine complementary European competencies with trusted global partners while retaining national regulatory responsibilities and safety standards.

From industrial ambition to industrial policy execution

The eight pillars describe the capabilities Europe needs. Turning them into an effective industrial strategy requires a delivery mechanism: identified owners, coordinated procurement signals, investment decisions, qualification pathways and evidence of progress. Without these elements, industrial policy can remain a collection of initiatives detached from the project schedule.

I would prioritise three practical instruments across European and national programmes. These are recommendations for programme design, not descriptions of mandatory EU instruments or an assumption that every Member State has the same nuclear objectives.

ACTION 01

Map critical capability and capacity

Establish an evidence-based view of suppliers, manufacturing capacity, specialist engineering, inspection, testing and fuel-related dependencies. Connect gaps to work packages, lead times and programme risk.

ACTION 02

Build qualification pathways

Connect training, supplier development, quality infrastructure, practical demonstrations and project-specific acceptance requirements. Help capable non-nuclear companies enter defined nuclear scopes without diluting safety requirements.

ACTION 03

Make the pipeline investable

Provide proportionate forward visibility of procurement packages, standardisation opportunities and upcoming capacity requirements. Link industrial-support decisions to credible milestones, not aspirational local-content targets.

Regulatory independence is non-negotiable. A regulator should not be made accountable for national-content percentages, supplier contracts or commercial outcomes. Industrial coordination must operate within, not around, the applicable safety and licensing framework.

Financing industrial capability before the first order

Nuclear-capable factories, specialist laboratories, advanced welding procedures, engineering tools and workforce qualifications require investment before revenue from a first project may materialise. That creates a familiar dilemma: companies need credible demand to invest, while projects need credible capacity before they can commit.

Policy and programme leaders should distinguish financing a reactor project from financing the industrial capability that makes it deliverable. Depending on national rules and the commercial case, possible mechanisms include co-investment in shared testing infrastructure, vocational programmes, supplier-development support, staged qualification work and bankable procurement visibility. Public involvement should be transparent, proportionate and compatible with applicable competition, procurement and State aid rules.

The objective is not to transfer every industrial risk to taxpayers or guarantee contracts for unqualified suppliers. It is to reduce avoidable uncertainty where commercially viable capability can be developed, while preserving competition, contractual accountability and nuclear safety.

Why requirements and nuclear quality belong inside industrial policy

European nuclear supply chains operate across different technologies, licensing bases, contractual systems and national regulatory frameworks. Some projects use U.S. nuclear technologies and associated requirements, while suppliers may be familiar primarily with European standards.

This is not simply a documentation issue. Terms such as Safety-Related, Important to Nuclear Safety, Commercial Grade Dedication, Design Authority and procurement flow-down can have project-specific technical and contractual consequences. Their meaning must be established within the applicable regulatory and procurement basis.

ISO 9001 or ISO 19443 certification may demonstrate important management-system characteristics, but it does not automatically establish compliance with every requirement of ASME NQA-1 or with the acceptance requirements for a specific safety-related scope. The actual obligations depend on the licensing basis, contracts, technical specifications and assigned work.

Industrial capability develops when organisations can interpret those obligations correctly and demonstrate that the resulting controls work in practice. Passing a general audit is not the same as demonstrating readiness for every nuclear delivery package.

Industrial readiness is scope-specific

Before assigning nuclear work, the programme should establish the applicable classification and requirements, supplier competence, oversight arrangements, acceptance criteria, evidence obligations and technical interfaces. The controls must be proportionate to the work and consistent with nuclear safety.

Technology transfer must reach the delivery organisation

Technology transfer is often described in terms of drawings, intellectual property, equipment and licences. These are important, but they are not sufficient. Nuclear projects also depend on engineering practices, configuration control, change management, procurement strategy, inspection methods and the ability to maintain a coherent safety and licensing basis.

For programmes introducing a technology into a new regulatory and industrial environment, the transfer of these practices should begin early. Otherwise, the nominally transferred design can remain dependent on external expertise for routine technical decisions, supplier qualification and future changes.

The better measure of technology transfer is whether the receiving organisations develop demonstrable capability to execute their responsibilities safely and reliably.

Industrial capability has to survive the full nuclear lifecycle

Industrial strategies often concentrate on construction because cranes, factories and new contracts are visible. But a nuclear facility may need qualified technical support for decades after first power. Long-term capability covers fuel-cycle services and fuel-supply resilience, spare parts, maintenance, modification engineering, periodic safety reviews, modernisation, inspection, digital and cyber resilience, and eventual decommissioning and waste management.

These stages do not carry identical requirements. They do share a need for competent organisations, configuration control, traceable technical information and an owner or operator able to maintain an effective safety and licensing basis. The supplier ecosystem must evolve as a plant progresses from construction to commissioning, operation and later life.

Planning only for the first construction peak risks losing knowledge and supplier capacity precisely when the facility enters its longest phase. A mature industrial strategy should show how training, facilities and contractual relationships will endure, and where capability can support the next unit, maintenance programmes or export markets.

Standardisation and digital tools should reduce friction

Repeatable reactor designs, shared component specifications and appropriate use of common supplier evidence can reduce unnecessary re-engineering and investment uncertainty. Digital engineering, model-based collaboration and controlled project information can strengthen interfaces from design to handover.

Neither standardisation nor software substitutes for technical judgement, configuration discipline or independent review. The useful question is whether the chosen approach allows a programme to control changes and demonstrate compliance more reliably, without disguising design differences or regulatory requirements.

A European approach with national accountability

Europe is not a single nuclear licensing jurisdiction. National regulators retain their responsibilities, and the appropriate requirements for a project cannot be replaced by a generic European checklist. At the same time, industry benefits when suppliers can recognise equivalent competencies, share lessons and avoid unnecessary duplication where compatible approaches are technically and legally appropriate.

Cross-border cooperation can strengthen manufacturing capacity, workforce mobility, technical services and qualification pathways. Cooperation with the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Japan, South Korea and other trusted partners remains valuable. Ukraine's nuclear engineering and operational experience can also contribute to the European knowledge base and workforce.

International cooperation should reinforce, rather than obscure, accountability for nuclear safety, design decisions and acceptance of work.

What should programme leaders measure?

An industrial strategy becomes credible when it is connected to delivery decisions and evidence. Programme owners and policymakers can track a limited number of meaningful questions:

  • Supplier readiness: How many organisations can demonstrate qualification for defined packages, rather than express interest in them?
  • Capacity and schedule: Which critical manufacturing, testing and engineering resources constrain the delivery pipeline?
  • Interface control: Are design, procurement, construction and regulatory responsibilities clearly allocated and actively managed?
  • Knowledge retention: Are project practices and lessons being captured so that later units require less reinvention?
  • Lifecycle and export value: Can the industrial capability support operations, maintenance, follow-on projects and international customers?
  • Investability: Do qualified suppliers have credible visibility of future packages, and are critical capability investments linked to defined project milestones?
  • Quality of execution: Are first-time acceptance, technical rework, documentation completion and supplier delivery performance improving from package to package?

The purpose is not to create another scorecard. It is to identify constraints early enough for organisations to act.

The relevance for Poland and Europe's new-build programmes

For Poland, the industrial strategy question goes beyond participation in one major nuclear construction programme. It is about developing a national base of engineering, construction, manufacturing, technical services, nuclear quality and project-management capability that can mature through successive phases and future projects.

The same logic applies to other European programmes, whether they involve large reactors, SMRs or the long-term support of existing plants. The technical and contracting models differ. The need for capable owners, clear interfaces, qualified suppliers and demonstrable safety performance does not. Poland's opportunity is to build capabilities that can contribute to large-reactor programmes, potential SMR deployment and cross-border markets, while recognising that each project has its own licensing and commercial basis.

That requires early alignment between the public programme framework, owner requirements, technology partners and domestic industry. A short-term focus on contract value alone would miss the longer-term prize: repeatable engineering and construction competence, qualified technical services and the ability to sustain operating facilities.

Bottom line

Europe's nuclear renaissance will not be defined only by announced gigawatts, technology selections or investment totals. It will be defined by whether nuclear projects can progress from commitment to controlled execution and then to safe, reliable operation.

An industrial strategy should turn individual projects into lasting capability. That means a credible pipeline, investable capacity, qualified suppliers, accountable organisations, clear requirements, effective integration and knowledge that remains after the first unit enters service. Success should be tested against evidence of repeatable performance, not declarations of interest.

Europe should measure nuclear industrial policy by the capabilities developed, qualified, integrated and retained across successive programmes, not by the number of companies expressing interest. Reactors can be ordered. Industrial capability must be developed, demonstrated and sustained.
